Modern embedding models are the result of a decade of progressive refinement. The lineage starts with Word2Vec (2013), which showed that a shallow neural network trained to predict neighboring words could learn word vectors with remarkable linear structure: king - man + woman ≈ queen. The trick was to treat the embedding table as the model, throwing the rest away.

GloVe (2014) and FastText (2016) refined the approach. GloVe used global co-occurrence statistics instead of a local window, which gave better quality on small corpora. FastText subword decomposition handled out-of-vocabulary words and morphologically rich languages by treating each word as a bag of character n-grams. Both still produced word-level vectors — you could not get a sentence vector without averaging, which loses word order.

The transformer era (BERT, 2018) changed the game. A BERT [CLS] token embedding is context-dependent, so 'bank' in 'river bank' and 'bank account' gets different vectors. But the [CLS] embedding is a poor sentence representation out of the box — it was trained for classification, not similarity. SBERT (2019) fixed this with a Siamese training objective: encode two sentences, compare them with cosine, and train to maximize the similarity of paraphrases. The result was the sentence-transformers library, which is still the default for off-the-shelf sentence embeddings in 2026.

The current frontier is general-purpose text embedding models trained on huge weakly-supervised pairs (query, document, label) — OpenAI text-embedding-3, BGE, E5, GTE, Cohere embed-v3. These models are tuned for retrieval specifically, so they outperform SBERT on benchmarks like MTEB by a wide margin. The trade-off is size (often multi-GB) and the loss of the small, fast, locally-runnable model that SBERT was famous for. For most retrieval-augmented generation pipelines, the choice of embedding model is the single biggest lever on quality.
